# Image Storytelling

## Build an Image Argument

Assign each image a role:

| Role | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Stakes | Who or what is affected? |
| Context | Where does the need or use occur? |
| Mechanism | What work, product, or service happens? |
| Proof | What credible evidence supports the claim? |
| Outcome | What relief, result, or transformation is visible? |
| Close | What human meaning should remain before action? |

Sequence images with the argument. Do not add near-duplicates only to make a
page feel longer.

## Captions

A caption is a mini-ad. It should state what can safely be observed, connect
the moment to the reader's reason to care, and avoid unproved identity,
location, outcome, or causation.

Bad: “Campaign materials showing a water distribution.”

Better: “Every prepared water pack carries the possibility of relief.”

## Image Evidence Ledger

Record file/URL, source, rights, consent, date/location, sensitivity, verified
facts, page role, alt text, crop, and mobile behavior. `UNKNOWN` is safer than
an invented caption.

## Rights, Consent, and Dignity

Public availability does not prove permission. Verify reuse rights and required
consent, especially for children, refugees, patients, or people in crisis.

When consent is unknown, prefer organization-owned or licensed images, adults,
field context, infrastructure, supplies, or non-identifying views. Avoid names,
invented stories, and distress used as decoration. Flag the publication gap.

## Practical Quality

- choose a relevant cover with a readable crop;
- preserve aspect ratio and optimize file weight;
- write useful alt text;
- check desktop and mobile crops;
- avoid baked-in text when real text is supported;
- never let an image contradict the adjacent claim.
